https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-03472-8 BMC Psychology *Correspondence: YunNan Zhao zynsasu@126.com Full list of author information is available at the end of the article Abstract Background Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) integrates mindfulness practices with cognitive strategies to alleviate perceived stress and its associated symptoms. While well studied in adults, evidence for schoolbased MBCT among adolescents with psychosomatic complaints—particularly in China—remains limited.

Methods We conducted a quasi-experimental, mixed-methods study in three public secondary schools in DaZhou (Sichuan, China). Female students aged 14–16 were screened (N = 1,200) using the Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS; cutoff > 45) and DSM-5–informed clinical interviews; 60 eligible students were randomly allocated to MBCT (8 weekly 75-minute sessions) or school-as-usual control. To mitigate non-specific effects, controls received attentionbalanced administrative contacts (weekly neutral check-ins without psychological content). Outcome assessors and the data analyst were blinded to allocation. The primary outcomes were the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS; α = 0.85) and the PCS (α = 0.86), both of which were adapted for Chinese adolescents. Parent (n = 6) and teacher (n = 5) interviews were analyzed thematically (Braun & Clarke).

Results Compared with controls, the MBCT group showed greater reductions in perceived stress and psychosomatic symptoms at post-test and two-month follow-up (Group×Time, p < .001; partial η²≈0.32–0.63). Mean reductions exceeded a conventional 0.5 SD benchmark, supporting applied relevance alongside statistical significance.

Qualitative analysis yielded six themes that contextualized change: (1) reduced stress reactivity, (2) improved emotion regulation and self-awareness, (3) strengthened coping, (4) enhanced peer/family communication, (5) better classroom engagement, and (6) conditions for maintenance (practice dose, prompts, family/teacher supports).

Conclusions School-delivered MBCT was associated with sustained improvements in perceived stress and psychosomatic complaints among 14–16-year-old girls, with convergent qualitative evidence from parents and teachers. Interpretation remains cautious given the passive control, short follow-up, and bounded generalizability.

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for perceived stress and psychosomatic symptoms in Chinese adolescent girls: a mixed-methods school-based study of parent and teacher perceptions YunNan Zhao1,2*

Introduction In recent years, the mental health of students with chronic emotional and psychosomatic disorders has become a growing concern among educators, families, and policymakers [1]. Among these students, the inter­ play between psychological stress and physical symp­ toms such as fatigue, headaches, sleep disturbances, and gastrointestinal issues often complicates diagnosis and intervention [2]. These manifestations are commonly observed in adolescents with undiagnosed or under-sup­ ported mental disorders, who experience heightened per­ ceived stress and emotional dysregulation in academic settings, which can further exacerbate their psychological and physical well-being [3]. To maintain conceptual clar­ ity and sampling precision, the present study focuses on Chinese secondary-school adolescents aged 14–16 years, and all references to the target group consistently use this age range.

Perceived stress—a subjective evaluation of environ­ mental demands exceeding one’s coping resources—has been identified as a major contributing factor to poor mental health in adolescents [4]. This form of stress not only intensifies existing emotional problems but also contributes to somatization, blurring the line between physical and psychological distress [5]. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to these effects due to ongoing developmental changes, making them more susceptible to stress-related disorders. Because school is a primary context of daily stress for 14–16-year-olds, effective sup­ port should emphasize emotion regulation and selfmanagement skills that are feasible within routine school schedules [6].

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) has emerged as a promising approach for improving emo­ tional regulation, reducing perceived stress, and enhanc­ ing mental health, particularly among adolescents with internalizing symptoms [7]. Originally developed to pre­ vent relapse in depression, MBCT integrates cognitive therapy with mindfulness techniques to cultivate presentmoment awareness and reduce automatic, maladaptive reactions to stress [8]. Recent trials indicate reductions in anxiety, depression, and perceived stress in student samples, alongside gains in attention, emotion regulation, and resilience [9, 10]. However, evidence specific to schooldelivered MBCT for Chinese adolescents aged 14–16 pre­ senting with psychosomatic complaints remains limited, indicating a clear implementation gap in this setting.

Students with psychosomatic and emotional disorders may struggle to articulate their distress, making adult observations valuable complements to self-report [11]. Teachers often detect behavioral changes in classrooms, while parents observe patterns at home [12]. In this study, we incorporate concise input from parents and teachers to complement adolescent self-reports and to gauge whether any improvements generalize across school and home con­ texts [13].

Although MBCT shows benefits for stress and emo­ tional health, few investigations have systematically examined how such interventions are appraised by the adults who support adolescents beyond the therapeutic context [14, 15]. Furthermore, adolescents with psycho­ somatic symptoms are underrepresented in intervention research, despite indications that perceived stress medi­ ates links between cognitive vulnerabilities and poor mental health outcomes [16, 17]. Addressing this evidence gap can refine tools tailored to the needs of 14–16-yearold students with psychosomatic complaints in Chinese schools [18].

Accordingly, this study evaluates a school-based MBCT program for Chinese adolescents aged 14–16 with psy­ chosomatic symptoms, testing its effectiveness in reducing perceived stress and improving mental health. Outcomes are triangulated using adolescent self-reports and stream­ lined evaluations from parents and teachers to estab­ lish ecological validity within everyday school and home environments.

Literature review Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) is com­ monly theorized as a dual-mechanism intervention that couples nonjudgmental, present-moment awareness with cognitive reappraisal to interrupt stress–affect–symptom cycles. This account is consistent with work on revers­ ing maladaptive neuroplasticity in persistent mood dis­ turbance [18] and with evidence that acceptanceand mindfulness-based self-help can reduce social anxiety symptoms when compared with inactive controls [19].

Neurodevelopmental findings further indicate that mind­ fulness practice modulates large-scale functional con­ nectivity in children and adolescents—changes plausibly linked to improved regulatory capacity relevant to psy­ chosomatic complaints [20]. Because change must gener­ alize beyond the therapy room, adjunctive programming for key adults (parents and teachers) is often emphasized Future work should test MBCT against attention-matched/active comparators, extend follow-up to 6–12 months, and examine mechanisms (e.g., decentering, rumination) and cultural/implementation outcomes to inform scaling in diverse Chinese school contexts.

to scaffold practice and reinforce coping in everyday set­ tings [21]. Adult clinical trials underscore efficacy while surfacing significant design contrasts. For example, a randomized comparison of MBCT versus active stress-management training in obsessive–compulsive disorder supports dis­ order-specific benefits beyond nonspecific stress relief [22]. In contrast, correlational and experimental work have connected mindfulness with self-compassion and well-being, even among non-meditators, suggesting broad transdiagnostic relevance [23]. Large-scale synthe­ ses demonstrate that mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) confer benefits across various psychiatric condi­ tions, although with notable heterogeneity by diagnosis, comparator, and setting [24–26]. Experimental para­ digms demonstrate accelerated recovery from induced negative affect following mindfulness training [27], and studies of trauma-exposed individuals highlight the roles of avoidance and dispositional mindfulness in internal­ izing symptoms—mechanistic targets directly addressed in MBCT [28]. Comparisons of metacognitive therapy and mindfulness meditation reveal process differences (explicit belief modification versus decentering and attentional training), clarifying which therapeutic ingre­ dients may be most important for somatic amplification [29]. Programmatic reflections on MBCT likewise call for clearer articulation of active components and implemen­ tation standards—concerns that inform adolescent adap­ tations [30].

Delivery format and context also shape outcomes. Tele-delivered MBCT shows feasibility and preliminary efficacy, illustrating how access barriers can be reduced without abandoning core elements [31]. Culturally diverse randomized trials (e.g., among depressed adults with disabilities in Nigeria) demonstrate that MBCT’s benefits are not limited to WEIRD samples, reinforcing the need for local adaptation rather than wholesale rein­ vention [32]. At the same time, studies in young adults have linked daily stress, affect, and health behaviors, underscoring the importance of targeting stress-reactive routines alongside symptoms [33]. Meanwhile, gen­ dered coping differences suggest potential moderation of intervention effects that developers should anticipate in school settings [34]. Population studies of suicide risk and eating-disorder symptomatology in college cohorts, together with evidence on high sedentary time and its mental-health correlates, further situate stress and health behaviors as intertwined targets—aspects often coaddressed by MBIs [35, 36]. Classic stress-process theory provides a framework for interpreting these multilevel influences and for designing multi-informant assess­ ments that capture cross-context generalization [37].

Evidence specific to psychosomatic presentations strengthens the rationale for MBCT in youth with somatic complaints. Randomized and clinical trials in irritable bowel syndrome have shown reduced symptom severity and improved quality of life following MBCT, with convergent theoretical implications for targeting interoceptive attention and catastrophic appraisal [38, 39]. Measurement choices matter when tracking cooccurring internalizing symptoms; validated tools used in primary care (e.g., PHQ-9) illustrate trade-offs between brevity and construct coverage that adolescent studies must weigh carefully [40]. Because coping resources and family support shape symptom expression and main­ tenance, contextual factors such as family functioning and stress burden are pertinent covariates or modera­ tors in school-based trials [41]. Financial and role-strain stressors also intersect with mental health in students, highlighting the importance of assessing a broader stress ecology when interpreting the effects of MBCT [42]. Rep­ licated gender patterns in coping advise attention to sub­ group responsiveness and potential tailoring [34]. Sleep, a frequent co-occurring complaint in psychosomatic profiles, is a relevant secondary endpoint in adolescent studies given its bidirectional links with stress and mood [43]. Preventive mental health efforts in scholastic set­ tings (e.g., eating disorder prevention) demonstrate that universal programs can be effective. Still, their effects depend on fit, fidelity, and the salience of outcomes for the target group [44]. Long-term maintenance observed in pain-related conditions (e.g., primary headache) sug­ gests that when practice continues, benefits can per­ sist—an argument for booster sessions and home–school practice bridges in youth protocols [45]. Contemporary psychosomatic psychiatry underscores mind–body bidi­ rectionality, reinforcing integrated designs that track both psychological and somatic endpoints [46]. Within China, stressors such as bullying and cyberbullying are linked to poorer mental health in adolescents, contextu­ alizing why school-embedded, developmentally sensitive MBCT may be warranted [47]; more broadly, research on populations facing major psychosocial stressors illus­ trates how context can complicate symptom interpreta­ tion and intervention transferability—cautionary notes when generalizing adult evidence to younger or culturally distinct groups [48].

Recent syntheses and adolescent-focused studies help refine adaptation priorities. Integrative and metaanalytic reviews call for sharper tests of mechanisms (e.g., decentering, rumination) and stress the need for age-appropriate dosing, active comparators, and lon­ ger follow-up in youth [49–51]. In China, a randomized classroom trial combining mindfulness with life skills training improved emotion regulation and reduced anxi­ ety in secondary students, demonstrating the feasibility of this approach in real-time schedules and highlighting the value of layered skills alongside mindfulness practice

[52]. Complementary school-delivered work in middleschoolers reported reductions in learning burnout, hint­ ing at academic relevance when protocols align with curricular rhythms [53]. Brief digital adaptations in Chi­ nese adolescents have demonstrated short-term prosocial benefits, offering a scalable adjunct to in-person sessions.

Meanwhile, meta-analytic evidence in Chinese college students supports anxiety reduction, but cautions against direct generalization to younger adolescents without developmental tailoring of framing, practice, and sup­ port [54, 55]. Extensive cluster-randomized evaluations of universal school mindfulness (e.g., MYRIAD; ~8,400 students) report no advantage over teaching-as-usual at one year, highlighting how instructor training, delivery mandate, and home practice can mute effects [56]. Other cluster designs with stronger fidelity support yield small but positive effects, and umbrella reviews emphasize cul­ tural/contextual adaptation, as well as implementation quality—key considerations for academically intensive Chinese secondary schools and for adolescents with psy­ chosomatic complaints whose symptoms fluctuate with stress exposure [57, 58].

Addressing the methodological concerns raised in prior work, our design (i) targets a clearly defined develop­ mental window (ages 14–16), (ii) uses a school-delivered MBCT protocol with instructor training and fidelity safe­ guards, (iii) specifies psychosomatic and stress outcomes a priori, (iv) incorporates multi-informant assessment (student, teacher, parent) to capture home–school gen­ eralization, and (v) situates interpretation within contex­ tual stress ecology [59, 60]. These choices are intended to move beyond cataloging efficacy toward a synthesis that tests theoretically specified mechanisms while attending to the cultural and developmental realities of Chinese secondary schools.

Objectives Guided by a convergent mixed-methods design, this study pursues three interlocking objectives. First, it will determine whether a school-delivered MBCT program produces greater reductions than a control condition in perceived stress and psychosomatic symptom burden among secondary-school adolescents aged 14–16, with “mental health” explicitly operationalized as Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) total and Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS) total/subscales measured at baseline, postintervention, and follow-up. Second, in parallel, it will qualitatively examine how students, parents, and teach­ ers perceive changes in emotion regulation, coping, classroom functioning, and somatic complaints, while also appraising the acceptability and feasibility of MBCT through semi-structured interviews. Finally, to move beyond parallel findings toward an integrated account, the study will synthesize quantitative and qualitative results—using joint displays and triangulation—to evalu­ ate the convergence between measured improvements (PSS/PCS) and stakeholder-reported changes, thereby interpreting outcomes through the study’s theoretical lens of attention regulation, decentering, and cognitive reappraisal within the everyday school ecology. Guided by the study aims, we state the following hypotheses: H1 MBCT produces a significant Group (MBCT vs. con­ trol) × Time (baseline, post-test, follow-up) interaction on the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and the Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS), with greater reductions in the MBCT group.

H2 After adjustment for baseline (and prespecified covariates), the MBCT group shows lower PSS and PCS means than the control group at post-test and follow-up. H3 A higher proportion of MBCT participants achieves clinically meaningful improvement on PSS and PCS (e.g., ≥ 0.5 SD reduction or reliable change) than controls.

H4 = parents and teachers report improvements in stu­ dents’ emotion regulation, coping, classroom functioning, and management of somatic complaints after MBCT rela­ tive to control. Method Sampling and design This study adopted a mixed-methods approach, inte­ grating a quasi-experimental quantitative design with a complementary qualitative component to examine the effectiveness and contextual perceptions of MindfulnessBased Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) for students with psychosomatic disorders. The research was conducted in DaZhou, SiChuan Province, during the 2022–2023 academic year and targeted female students in the sec­ ond stage of secondary education. To eliminate prior inconsistencies and protect internal validity, the target age range is standardized to 14–16 years throughout the study. A two-phase selection process was employed: first, stratified random sampling recruited 1,200 students from multiple public secondary schools; second, purposive selection identified those who met the inclusion criteria.

In the quantitative phase, a pretest–posttest controlgroup design with a two-month follow-up was employed. The Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS) was adminis­ tered to all 1,200 students; those scoring above the clini­ cal threshold (score > 45) were shortlisted. From this pool, 70 students completed clinical interviews with trained school psychologists in line with DSM-5 criteria; 60 stu­ dents were eligible and randomly assigned to the experi­ mental (n = 30) or control group (n = 30) via computerized randomization executed by a researcher independent of

data collection. Allocation concealment used sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) prepared by an independent coordinator. Because participants nec­ essarily know whether they attend sessions, participant blinding to the condition is not feasible. Instead, we blind them to the study hypotheses and the expected direction of effects. We also blind outcome assessors and the data ana­ lyst to group allocation to minimize detection bias.

An a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1) indicated that 26 participants per group provide 0.80 power to detect a medium effect (f = 0.25) at α = 0.05; thus, the randomized sample is adequate. The experimental group received MBCT in eight weekly 75-minute sessions delivered by a licensed mindfulness practitioner; fidelity was monitored via a standardized MBCT adherence checklist. The con­ trol group continued their usual school schedule with­ out added programming. Because the control condition is passive, we explicitly acknowledge the risks of expectancy and attention (placebo) effects. To attenuate these, assess­ ment contact, scheduling communications, and incentives were standardized across groups; however, non-specific effects cannot be entirely excluded and are treated as a design limitation a priori. Three participants withdrew (two experimental, one control), leaving 57 completers (28 experimental, 29 control).

Eligibility required: PCS > 45; no current psychiatric medication/psychotherapy; age 14–16 years; student assent and guardian consent; and commitment to ses­ sion attendance. Exclusion criteria were > 3 absences, disqualifying medical conditions, or refusal to complete follow-up. Group means did not differ at baseline on key measures.

To enrich interpretation, a qualitative case-study strand was purposively sampled, comprising six parents (three mothers, three fathers) of experimental-group students and five teachers (two counselors, three subject teachers).

Semi-structured interviews were conducted ~ 1 week after the intervention, lasting 30–45 min, and took place in private rooms at the school. The guide elicited obser­ vations on behavioral, emotional, and academic change; implementation challenges; and recommendations for school deployment. Data adequacy is defined a priori as thematic saturation (no new codes/themes across two con­ secutive interviews); saturation was reached within the 11 interviews, and an audit trail was maintained to support dependability.

Instruments To gather robust data across both quantitative and quali­ tative phases, this study employed two standardized selfreport questionnaires and a semi-structured interview protocol. The instruments were selected for their con­ ceptual relevance, previous psychometric validation, and suitability for use with Chinese adolescent populations.

Where necessary, cultural and linguistic adaptations were made to enhance contextual appropriateness, and all tools underwent preliminary pilot testing to assess clar­ ity, relevance, and cultural fit.

Psychosomatic complaints scale (PCS) The Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS), developed by Takada and Sakata [61], is designed to assess psy­ chosomatic symptoms in adolescents. It consists of 30 items, each scored on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from “never” (0) to “repeatedly” (3), with total scores rang­ ing from 0 to 90. A cutoff score of 45 indicates poten­ tial psychosomatic dysfunction. Initial studies reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.64 and confirmed a unifactorial structure with one factor explaining 34.1% of the vari­ ance [61].

Subsequent standardization of the scale by Fruehwirth [62] confirmed its content validity through expert panel review and concurrent validity with the Goldberg Gen­ eral Health Scale. Hajloo [63] conducted exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses in an Iranian context, con­ firming its one-dimensionality (KMO = 0.782) and high reliability (α = 0.85; r = .85 for test–retest reliability). In the present study, the PCS underwent further psycho­ metric evaluation, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 and acceptable model fit indices in confirmatory factor analy­ sis (CFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.06), thus empirically support­ ing its reliability and validity for the Chinese adolescent sample. Before the main data collection, the PCS was forwardand back-translated by bilingual experts and reviewed by two Chinese clinical psychologists to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness. To strengthen face validity, beyond the original 10-student pilot, the adapted version was tested with 25 non-participating stu­ dents representative of the main sample, leading to the rephrasing of two ambiguous items.

Perceived stress scale (PSS) The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), developed by Cohen et al. [64], consists of 14 items, each rated on a 5-point scale (0 = “never” to 4 = “very often”), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 56. Higher scores reflect greater per­ ceived stress. Internal consistency in the original devel­ opment sample ranged from 0.84 to 0.86 [65]. Broton et al. [42] validated the scale cross-culturally, achieving acceptable reliability (α = 0.81 in the Iranian sample and 0.86 in the American sample).

In the present study, the PSS was adapted using the same rigorous forward–backward translation procedure applied to the PCS. Its content validity was reviewed by a panel of local mental health experts with experience in assessing adolescent stress. Cognitive interviews were conducted with 15 students to evaluate the semantic clar­ ity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension of the

items. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.85, con­ firming satisfactory internal consistency. A confirmatory factor analysis further validated the two-factor structure of the PSS (CFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.05).

Semi-structured interview protocol To support the qualitative aims, a semi-structured inter­ view protocol was developed based on themes drawn from MBCT literature, school-based intervention stud­ ies, and adolescent psychosocial frameworks. The guide included 10 open-ended items addressing emotional, behavioral, and social changes, perceived effectiveness of MBCT, observed challenges, and contextual fit in school settings.

To establish content validity, the protocol was reviewed by two qualitative research specialists and two school psychologists with experience in adolescent mental health. Pilot testing involved one parent and one teacher, resulting in minor revisions to improve the clarity and sensitivity of the wording. While no psychometric indi­ ces apply to open-ended protocols, trustworthiness was enhanced through expert validation, member checking after transcription, and adherence to Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis procedure [66]. The final interview pro­ tocol was designed to elicit nuanced reflections on both individual and contextual factors affecting the MBCT experience (See appendix).

Intervention We delivered an eight-session MBCT program (75 min per week) aligned with the standard manual and adapted for secondary school adolescents. Each session integrated a core mindfulness practice (e.g., breath/body awareness, mindful eating), targeted cognitive exercises (working with distressing thoughts and emotions, decentering), and a brief group discussion to consolidate learning.

Licensed mental-health professionals with formal MBCT training facilitated all sessions. We verified facilitator cre­ dentials before delivery, provided biweekly supervision throughout the trial, and monitored adherence using an MBCT fidelity checklist, structured observation notes, and facilitator logs.

Home practice was integral. Students received daily exercises and a brief reflective journal. We tracked adher­ ence through weekly self-reports and facilitator notes. When practice fell below four of seven days in a week, facilitators conducted a ≤ 10-minute motivational checkin to identify barriers, offered 3–5-minute “micro-prac­ tices,” and set a concrete goal for the following week; repeated shortfall over two consecutive weeks prompted tailored adjustments (shorter practices, alternate times of day). All deviations and resolutions were logged and later treated as fidelity covariates in sensitivity analyses.

The control condition was school-as-usual (passive comparator). Because passive controls can introduce non-specific influences (expectancy/attention effects), we implemented attention-balancing procedures a priori: both groups received identical administrative contact (weekly scheduling and well-being check-ins, ≤ 10 min with no psychological content), all study communica­ tions used neutral language, and the control group was offered delayed MBCT after the trial. To minimize con­ tamination, intervention and control activities occurred in separate rooms and time slots, and staff not involved in delivery were instructed not to discuss session content with students. We acknowledge that non-specific effects cannot be fully eliminated without an attention-matched or active control; consequently, we have pre-specified these limitations in the Methods section and planned an active, education-based comparator for future iterations.

Procedure We obtained approvals from the DaZhou (SiChuan) education authority and the university ethics board (Approval No. 2022-EDU-MBCT-18) and coordinated with three public girls’ secondary schools. Screening proceeded in two steps: first, the Psychosomatic Com­ plaints Scale (PCS) was administered to 1,200 students; second, those scoring > 45 completed DSM-5–based clinical interviews with trained school psychologists.

Sixty eligible adolescents (aged 14–16) were randomized (30 MBCT; 30 control) using a computerized sequence prepared by an independent coordinator. Allocation concealment was used sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Because students necessarily knew whether they attended sessions, we blinded the outcome assessors and the data analyst to allocation and provided participants with neutral study aims to limit expectancy effects.

The timeline comprised (1) baseline assessment, (2) the eight-week intervention, and (3) post-test plus a two-month follow-up. Sessions ran after school in quiet, ventilated classrooms. We maintained fidelity logs and selective audio recordings to verify adherence to the manual. The control group followed the routine timetable without added programming; both groups received the matched administrative contact described above. Attri­ tion was low (three withdrawals), and an attrition analy­ sis showed no baseline differences between completers and non-completers.

All procedures were free of charge. We obtained writ­ ten assent from students and consent from guardians, assigned anonymized IDs, and reported only aggregated data. COVID-19 precautions (distancing, masking, ven­ tilation, and sanitization) were applied uniformly across groups; no sessions were cancelled or moved online, and

there was no differential attrition or missingness attribut­ able to these measures. For quantitative outcomes, we used mixed ANOVA (Group × Time) with assumption checks and Bonferroniadjusted pairwise comparisons, reporting partial η² and confidence intervals. To strengthen missing-data han­ dling beyond intention-to-treat and last-observationcarried-forward, we employed multiple imputation as the primary strategy (m = 20; wholly conditional specifica­ tion under MAR) and reported LOCF and complete-case analyses as sensitivity checks; planned covariates (base­ line PSS/PCS, age, school) were included in adjusted models. For qualitative data, we conducted semi-struc­ tured interviews with parents and teachers ~ 1 week postintervention. We analyzed the transcripts in MAXQDA, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach, with dual independent coding and negotiated agreement. We used member checking with a subset of participants and documented thematic saturation and decision trails in an audit log. In addition, after completing the quantitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 11 selected informants, comprising 6 parents (3 mothers and 3 fathers) and 5 teachers (2 school counselors and 3 subject-area teachers). The purpose was to gather quali­ tative data on their perceptions of MBCT. The selection of the informants was based on their direct involvement with the students in the experimental group, ensuring a range of perspectives from both home and school settings. The interviews were designed to explore their observations, experiences, and opinions regarding the effects of MBCT on students’ perceived stress levels and psychological symptoms. Interviews were conducted in a private room at the school, lasting between 30 and 45 min. They were audio-recorded with the participants’ informed consent.

The interview process allowed participants to share their perspectives openly and provide detailed accounts of their observations. To ensure rigor in the qualitative analysis, a team of two independent researchers con­ ducted the coding process using Braun and Clarke’s the­ matic analysis framework. Both coders were blinded to the participants’ group status (experimental or control) and the study’s hypotheses to minimize potential bias from the interviewer. Triangulation was employed by comparing the perspectives of parents, teachers, and the students’ quantitative outcomes to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings. Data saturation was reached after the 11 interviews, as no new themes emerged during the analysis, indicating that sufficient data had been collected to repre­ sent the range of participant experiences.

Potential interviewer bias and reflexivity were addressed through regular team discussions, where coders reflected on their positionality and ensured that their pre­ conceptions did not influence the coding process.

Data analysis We analyzed quantitative and qualitative data using pro­ cedures aligned with the study aims and best practices for credibility and transparency. Quantitative phase We conducted analyses in SPSS 26.0 (two-tailed α = 0.05; 95% CIs). After descriptive summaries (means, SDs, fre­ quencies), we screened assumptions and data quality: visual outlier checks (boxplots; |z| >3 flagged for review), Shapiro–Wilk for normality, Levene for homogeneity, and Mauchly for sphericity (with Greenhouse–Geisser corrections where violated). Primary tests used a mixed 2 (Group: MBCT, control) × 3 (Time: pre, post, followup) ANOVA to estimate main and interaction effects.

Where interactions were significant, we ran Bonferroniadjusted pairwise contrasts; we reported partial η² for omnibus effects and Hedges g with 95% CIs for pairwise comparisons. Because baseline severity and background factors can influence change, we ran adjusted models that con­ trolled for baseline PSS/PCS, age, and school (fixed effect). In addition, to test robustness beyond repeatedmeasures ANOVA, we fit linear mixed-effects mod­ els (random intercept for participant; fixed Group, Time, Group×Time; same covariates) via SPSS MIXED.

Results from mixed models converged with ANOVA in sign and inference; we report both in the Results (pri­ mary = ANOVA; mixed models = sensitivity). Missingness was low (< 5%) and passed Little’s MCAR test; nevertheless, given known biases of LOCF, we used multiple imputation as our primary strategy: m = 20 data­ sets via fully conditional specification (predictive mean matching for continuous variables), including Group, Time, outcomes at all waves, and covariates in the impu­ tation model. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

We performed LOCF and complete-case analyses as sensitivity checks; the conclusions remained unchanged across both approaches. Where assumptions were mate­ rially violated, we confirmed patterns using heterosce­ dasticity-robust CIs and rank-based alternatives; these checks did not alter substantive interpretations.

Qualitative phase Interview audio was transcribed verbatim and imported into MAXQDA 2022. We used Braun and Clarke’s sixphase thematic analysis with a combined deductive– inductive codebook: deductive nodes reflected the study lens (attention regulation, decentering, cognitive reap­ praisal; classroom and home transfer), while inductive nodes captured participant-generated concepts. Two analysts independently coded an initial 20% of tran­ scripts, refined code definitions through memoing, and proceeded only after achieving Cohen’s κ ≥ 0.75 on over­ lapping segments. The remaining transcripts were dou­ ble-coded in rotating pairs; disagreements were resolved via negotiated agreement in weekly meetings, with deci­ sions logged in an audit trail (versioned codebook, deci­ sion memos, and rationale).

We used MAXQDA’s Code Matrix Browser and Sum­ mary Grid to compare patterns across informant groups (parents vs. teachers) and to support triangulation with quantitative change (theme–outcome matrices linking interview excerpts to participants’ PSS/PCS trajectories).

Member checking (with a subset of participants) and peer debriefing (with a methodologist not on the cod­ ing team) enhanced credibility; reflexivity memos docu­ mented the analysts’ positionality and decisions.

Saturation was operationalized a priori as the point at which no new codes or subthemes emerged in two con­ secutive interviews. We tracked this with a saturation grid; saturation was reached within the 11 interviews, supporting sample adequacy for our focused aims. The final themes prioritized semantic coherence and rel­ evance to the research questions (emotion regulation, coping, classroom functioning, and somatic complaint management), and were integrated with the quantitative results in joint displays to evaluate convergence and illu­ minate mechanisms.

Notably, no participant attri­ tion occurred between post-test and follow-up, ensuring consistency in sample size and enhancing the reliability of longitudinal comparisons.

Reduction in perceived stress This theme, reported by 10 of the 11 participants, reflects the intervention’s immediate impact on emotional regu­ lation and stress reactivity. Parents and teachers observed that students developed a calmer demeanor and were better equipped to manage academic and social pres­ sures. This observation aligns with previous research indicating that mindful­ ness alone may be insufficient for students with severe avoidance behaviors unless combined with additional cognitive-behavioral support. Interpreting this variation, students with high baseline avoidance may need graded exposure or brief skills coaching to translate mindful awareness into approach behavior; in our sample, such cases were also those with smaller PSS/PCS gains, indi­ cating partial convergence between qualitative accounts and quantitative change.

Improved psychological well-being Nine participants reported that students demonstrated improved mood, increased emotional resilience, and heightened self-awareness. Parents described their chil­ dren as “happier” and “more optimistic,” while teach­ ers noted greater emotional insight and articulation of feelings. These outcomes appear to stem from MBCT’s emphasis on observing thoughts and emotions with­ out judgment, which helped students reframe negative experiences.

Parent 3 stated, “My child’s mood has improved notice­ ably since starting MBCT. They seem happier and more optimistic about the future.” Teacher 1 observed, “Stu­ dents can now explain what they feel—some even say, ‘I’m overwhelmed,’ which they never said before.” Still, Parent 2 noted, “Although my child is more selfaware, I wouldn’t say they’re emotionally resilient yet—it’s a work in progress.” This variation highlights that while MBCT fosters self-awareness early on, emotional resil­ ience might require longer-term or reinforced practice to solidify. Consistent with the study’s mechanism-of-change lens, early gains in metacognitive awareness (decenter­ ing, labeling) likely precede durable affective resilience; this sequencing helps explain why some students showed strong mood reports before significant academic or social shifts.

Enhanced social relationships This theme, evident in 8 interviews, captures how MBCT facilitated empathy and improved peer interac­ tions. Teachers reported that students were more atten­ tive listeners and demonstrated greater patience and understanding during group activities. Parents simi­ larly observed that their children were more willing to share experiences and engage in conversations, which improved family communication dynamics.

Parent 5 shared, “I’ve noticed my child becoming more open and communicative since starting MBCT. They lis­ ten more attentively and show genuine concern for their friends.” Teacher 3 noted, “Students who practiced mind­ fulness exercises have become more empathetic and sup­ portive of classmates.” Yet, Teacher 5 pointed out, “Some students remain socially reserved despite the intervention,” indicating that while MBCT may enhance social-emotional aware­ ness, individual personality factors, such as introversion or past social experiences, may moderate its impact. This suggests a need for complementary social skills training for students who remain reluctant to engage with peers.

Accordingly, we interpret social gains as contingent on trait introversion and prior peer history; where reticence persisted, the corresponding students typically showed modest improvements in PSS/PCS, again aligning themelevel narratives with measured outcomes.

Improved academic engagement and performance Seven participants mentioned that MBCT contributed to enhanced focus, attention, and persistence in academic tasks. Teachers reported noticeable improvements in students’ ability to remain on task and engage actively in classroom discussions. These outcomes can be attributed to mindfulness techniques that promote attentional con­ trol and reduce cognitive interference caused by stress or negative emotions.

Parent 4 commented, “My child’s grades have improved since they started MBCT. They’re able to concentrate better and stay focused during study sessions.” Teacher 1 added, “Students who have been practicing mindfulness show more enthusiasm for learning and participate more actively.” However, some parents felt these gains were gradual.

Parent 6 remarked, “The academic improvements are there, but they’re slower compared to changes in mood or behavior.” This observation aligns with research indi­ cating that cognitive and academic benefits from mind­ fulness typically emerge later than emotional outcomes, as they require sustained cognitive retraining. This lag is consistent with a cascading-change account: stabilization of affect and stress first reduces interference (reflected in PSS/PCS), with study habits and performance consolidat­ ing more slowly under classroom demands.

Strengthened stress management skills Nine participants described how students became more adept at identifying stress triggers and employing mind­ ful strategies to manage their stress. Teachers highlighted improvements in problem-solving abilities, while parents noted that their children paused and reflected before reacting to stressful events. These skills suggest that MBCT’s emphasis on meta-awareness and non-reactivity effectively translated into adaptive coping.

Parent 2 shared, “My child has become more aware of their stress triggers and knows how to manage them better now.” Teacher 4 observed, “Students demonstrate better problem-solving skills and are more resourceful in dealing with challenges.” Yet, Teacher 2 noted challenges in habit formation: “Some students say they forget to use the techniques when stressed, which shows that reinforcement might still be needed.” This highlights the potential value of booster sessions or mobile-based mindfulness remind­ ers in helping students maintain consistent practice. We interpret these lapses as state-dependent retrieval fail­ ures; low-burden prompts (teacher cues, brief reminders) and booster sessions are plausible implementation levers to support skill access under peak arousal.

Sustained behavioral change and long-term mindfulness integration Six participants reported evidence of behavioral changes persisting beyond the intervention, including indepen­ dent mindfulness practice and improved emotional bal­ ance. Parent 1 shared, “Even after the program ended, my child continues to practice mindfulness regularly. It’s part of their daily routine.” Teacher 3 commented, “Students report feeling more grounded and continue using tech­ niques beyond the sessions.” However, not all students maintained these habits. Par­ ent 6 admitted, “He used to meditate daily, but now only does it when reminded.” This variability suggests that while MBCT can initiate long-term behavioral shifts, sustained reinforcement and support from parents or teachers are critical for habit consolidation. We theorize that maintenance is a function of practice “dose,” environ­ mental scaffolds, and perceived usefulness. Students who reported routine supports (parent prompts, peer practice) also tended to show larger sustained quantitative gains, indicating cross-strand convergence.

Discussion and conclusion This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of mind­ fulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) in reducing perceived stress and improving mental health among adolescents with psychosomatic disorders. The findings suggest that MBCT had a significant positive impact on reducing perceived stress and improving mental health, aligning with previous studies supporting mindfulness interventions for psychological distress [1, 2]. In applied terms, the observed mean reductions exceeded a conven­ tional half-standard-deviation benchmark in this sample, which strengthens the case for clinical relevance; however, this threshold is heuristic and should be interpreted cau­ tiously alongside confidence intervals. However, the small sample size, quasi-experimental design, and lack of an active control group limit the generalizability of these findings. These factors, alongside the absence of blind­ ing, may have contributed to potential placebo effects; therefore, future studies should incorporate larger, ran­ domized controlled trials to isolate the specific effects of MBCT more effectively. Specifically, expectancy and attention effects could inflate between-group contrasts when controls receive less contact; detection bias is also plausible when assessors are not fully blinded. These risks may bias estimates upward, implying that our effects rep­ resent an upper bound under these design constraints.

The primary outcome of reduced perceived stress found in this study corroborates findings by Porcelli et al. [3], who demonstrated that mindfulness interven­ tions can help alleviate stress in adolescents with psy­ chosomatic disorders. Adolescents with such disorders often face heightened vulnerability to stress due to the

chronic interaction between physical symptoms and negative cognitive patterns [4]. MBCT appears to dis­ rupt this cycle by promoting metacognitive awareness, enabling adolescents to observe their thoughts, emo­ tions, and bodily sensations as transient events rather than fixed realities. Although these results are promis­ ing, the absence of an active control group and the lack of blinding warrant caution in interpreting the efficacy of MBCT, as placebo effects could not be ruled out. This mindfulness approach helps patients disengage from maladaptive thinking patterns, reducing the intensity of stress responses. Fundamentally, mindfulness fosters acceptance and a non-judgmental awareness of thoughts, which promotes cognitive, metacognitive, and behavioral strategies that focus attention on the present moment rather than on worries or future stressors [5]. Future tri­ als should test theorized mechanisms (e.g., changes in decentering, rumination, and resilience) as mediators of PSS/PCS change and examine moderators such as base­ line avoidance, trait introversion, home-practice dose, and teacher fidelity to clarify for whom and under what condi­ tions MBCT works best.

The findings of this study are also consistent with recent evidence supporting the effectiveness of mind­ fulness-based interventions in reducing psychological distress and improving emotion regulation. For exam­ ple, Feng et al. [67] demonstrated that mindfulness sig­ nificantly reduced parenting stress among parents of children with autism, with resilience and psychological flexibility mediating this effect. Similarly, Zhang et al.

[68] showed that mindfulness helped Chinese university students manage academic stress and improve emotional intelligence. A meta-analysis by Ng et al. [69] confirmed that mindfulness-based interventions are highly effective in reducing psychological symptoms in high school ado­ lescents. Additionally, Lan et al. [70] found that mindful­ ness combined with life-skills training helped improve emotion regulation and reduce anxiety symptoms among Chinese migrant children. This body of research rein­ forces the notion that MBCT provides a robust frame­ work for addressing both emotional and psychosomatic challenges in adolescents. However, it would be benefi­ cial to incorporate cultural context into future studies to assess whether these interventions are equally effective in different cultural settings, particularly in how Chinese adolescents respond to such interventions. In particu­ lar, collectivist norms, academic intensity, family–school coordination, and stigma surrounding help-seeking may shape both uptake and outcomes. Process evaluations that document cultural tailoring (such as language, met­ aphors, and parental involvement) and measure accept­ ability/appropriateness will improve interpretability and transferability.

These therapeutic features suggest that MBCT encour­ ages individuals to reconsider and reinterpret stress­ ful experiences, reducing cognitive distortions such as catastrophizing and overgeneralization [10]. Moreover, MBCT’s focus on being present in the moment helps individuals shift attention away from stress-inducing thoughts toward sensory experiences—such as bodily sensations, sounds, or mental images—thus alleviat­ ing the cognitive load caused by stress [5]. While these mechanisms are promising, further research is needed to explore how these cognitive and emotional changes evolve, particularly beyond the short-term follow-up period in this study. Longer follow-ups (e.g., 6–12 months) with maintenance/booster supports and repeated media­ tor assessments would clarify durability and temporal sequencing of change.

The second significant finding concerns the improve­ ment in mental health outcomes for students with psy­ chosomatic disorders. This aligns with findings from Lan et al. [9], Petrenko et al. [2], and Martin et al. [11], who emphasized MBCT’s ability to enhance emotional resil­ ience and reduce psychosomatic symptoms. By equip­ ping individuals with cognitive and behavioral strategies, MBCT helps students manage stress and anxiety, thereby improving their ability to function effectively in daily life.

Mindfulness techniques are known to reduce emotional distress and alleviate physical symptoms associated with psychosomatic conditions [12]. Through mechanisms such as enhanced body awareness and cognitive refram­ ing, MBCT reduces avoidance behaviors and encourages acceptance of symptoms as part of a stress-response sys­ tem, thereby reducing their severity. While these findings are significant, the absence of a placebo or active control group, as well as the lack of blinding in this study, are substantial limitations that could affect the robustness of the conclusions. Moreover, the relative stability of the control group could reflect valid invariance, regression to the mean, or minor expectancy effects from administrative contact; an attention-matched or education-based com­ parator would help disentangle specific from non-specific benefits.

A key consideration in this study is the cultural context, as the sample consisted of Chinese adolescents. While the intervention’s effectiveness in reducing stress and improving mental health is evident, the cultural context is not adequately explored in this study. Given the dis­ tinct cultural values and practices in China, it is essen­ tial to understand how these factors may influence both the effectiveness and acceptability of the intervention.

Future research should investigate how cultural factors— such as collectivism, attitudes toward mental health, and social expectations—affect the success of mindfulnessbased interventions in non-Western contexts. We recom­ mend prespecifying culturally relevant implementation

outcomes (acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness), doc­ umenting adaptations using standardized frameworks, and conducting multi-site studies to test transportability across provinces and school types.

In conclusion, this study contributes valuable insights into the potential of MBCT to reduce stress and improve mental health among adolescents with psychosomatic symptoms. By integrating both quantitative outcomes and qualitative insights from parents and teachers, the research offers a comprehensive view of the impact of MBCT on adolescents’ well-being. However, the study’s limitations, such as the small sample size, the quasiexperimental design, and the lack of an active control group, suggest that these findings should be interpreted with caution. Generalizability is bounded by setting (three public schools in DaZhou), age (14–16), and gen­ der (female), and the design likely overestimates effects relative to fully blinded, attention-matched RCTs. To strengthen the validity of future research, studies should utilize randomized controlled trials, include larger and more diverse samples, and incorporate extended followup periods to assess the durability of the intervention’s effects. Additionally, psychological mechanisms under­ lying the efficacy of MBCT should be explored in more detail, focusing on the cognitive and emotional processes involved in these interventions. Specifically, we advocate for multi-arm trials comparing MBCT with an attentionmatched health education control, preregistered mediator analyses (decentering, rumination, resilience), and moder­ ator analyses (baseline avoidance, practice dose, instruc­ tor fidelity), as well as cost-effectiveness assessments, to inform school policy. Finally, the cultural context should be carefully considered, particularly when implementing MBCT in non-Western populations, to ensure that the intervention is both practical and culturally appropriate.

Adopting a cautious interpretive stance until such trials are complete will prevent overgeneralization and guide responsible scaling. Practical implications Given the demonstrated benefits of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) in reducing perceived stress and improving mental health outcomes, integrating MBCT into school-based psychological services appears to be a promising avenue for enhancing adolescent well-being. However, the real-world implementation of MBCT in educational settings requires addressing several systemic and logistical challenges. One signifi­ cant barrier is the limited availability of trained coun­ selors in schools. Many schools already face a shortage of mental health professionals, and adding MBCT as a standard intervention could stretch existing resources.

To overcome this, schools could explore partnerships with external mental health professionals or community organizations to provide additional support, especially in underserved areas. As a concrete pathway, districts can adopt a “train-the-trainer” model: (i) certify 2–3 lead counselors per school (24–32 h of MBCT-specific training plus supervised practice), (ii) cascade 6–8 h of basic mind­ fulness classroom skills to homeroom teachers, and (iii) schedule monthly supervision huddles to maintain fidelity and address implementation drift. Staffing plans should specify caseload ratios (e.g., ≤ 12 students per facilitator per cycle), release time for teachers, and coverage for ses­ sions to minimize academic disruption.

Time constraints within academic schedules present another challenge. School schedules are already packed with core educational content, and finding time for MBCT sessions could be difficult. To address this, MBCT could be integrated into existing programs such as socialemotional learning (SEL) curricula or offered as part of extracurricular activities. Another option could be offer­ ing short, weekly mindfulness sessions during breaks or advisory periods, which would allow students to engage in MBCT without disrupting their academic studies. The development of flexible, brief interventions that require minimal time commitment from students could increase feasibility and adoption in schools. Operationally, schools can pilot two delivery formats and compare feasibility: (a) the full protocol (8 × 75 min after school) versus (b) a micro-dose format (10 × 20 min embedded in advisory/ SEL blocks), with the latter supplemented by 5-minute “transition practices” at the start of classes. A scheduling matrix that avoids peak exam periods and allows makeup sessions will reduce attrition.

Additionally, to effectively implement MBCT, there is a need for culturally adapted training materials and resources. As highlighted by recent studies, cultural fac­ tors play an essential role in the acceptability and effec­ tiveness of psychological interventions, particularly in diverse educational settings [1]. In the context of nonWestern populations, like Chinese adolescents, mind­ fulness interventions may require adjustments to better align with local cultural values and beliefs about mental health. Developing culturally relevant training programs for counselors and teachers would ensure that MBCT resonates with students and meets their unique psy­ chological and social needs. For example, incorporat­ ing local beliefs about stress, emotional regulation, and coping could enhance the intervention’s relevance and impact. Illustratively, scripts can use culturally resonant metaphors (e.g., 调息/tiao-xi for “regulating breath,” 修 身/xiūshēn for self-cultivation), pair practices with col­ lectivist values (classroom harmony, filial respect), and include family-friendly home activities that fit examheavy routines (e.g., 3-minute evening body scan before homework). Teacher materials should provide bilingual key terms, examples from Chinese classroom life (e.g.,

recitation pressure), and brief guidance for stigma-sen­ sitive discussions about mental health. Where possible, parent orientation slides and WeChat mini-modules can normalize practice and invite supportive routines at home.

A feasible next step for the broader implementation of MBCT in schools could be the development of pilot pro­ grams and workshops for teachers and counselors. These pilot programs could serve as testing grounds for evaluat­ ing the scalability of MBCT in different educational set­ tings. By running small-scale trials, schools can assess the practical challenges of implementation, gather feedback from participants, and fine-tune the delivery of MBCT to maximize its effectiveness and accessibility. Teachers and counselors could receive specialized training on how to facilitate mindfulness exercises and incorporate MBCT principles into their daily interactions with students.

These programs could also help familiarize school staff with mindfulness-based strategies, which could be ben­ eficial in fostering a supportive school climate that pri­ oritizes mental health. We recommend a 6–9-month pilot framework with clear milestones: Month 1—staff training and fidelity rehearsal; Months 2–4—first student cohort (randomized to MBCT vs. attention-matched health edu­ cation), Months 5–6—second cohort; Months 7–9—data synthesis and scale-up planning. Monitor both effective­ ness (PSS/PCS pre/post/follow-up) and implementa­ tion outcomes (acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness, fidelity), and include teacher-rated classroom behavior as a pragmatic metric. Supervision checklists, session obser­ vation rubrics, and brief student practice logs will help maintain fidelity while identifying training gaps.

To further support students’ independent practice and enhance the sustainability of intervention effects, schools could incorporate digital mindfulness tools, such as guided audio exercises, mobile applications, or online platforms. These tools could provide students with addi­ tional resources to practice mindfulness outside of sched­ uled sessions, reinforcing the skills learned during MBCT and encouraging self-regulation. Digital tools provide an accessible and scalable solution to support the ongo­ ing practice of mindfulness, particularly in schools where time and staffing resources are limited. Mobile apps with mindfulness exercises could also help engage students who might be reluctant to participate in traditional faceto-face sessions, providing them with a more private and flexible means of integrating mindfulness into their daily lives. Moreover, these tools can offer real-time tracking and feedback, providing valuable insights into students’ progress and enabling educators to personalize inter­ ventions as needed. In the Chinese school context, lowfriction options include QR-coded audio libraries hosted on the school’s LMS and WeChat mini-programs with 3–5 min practices, offline access for bandwidth-limited students, and opt-in push reminders before study periods.

Aggregated, de-identified usage analytics (practice “dose”) can be linked to outcomes to inform targeted supports, while privacy protocols should be aligned with local regu­ lations and school policies. Given the qualitative evidence for the role of families, brief parent micro-modules (e.g., “how to prompt without pressuring”) and at-home dyadic practices can extend benefits beyond the classroom and improve habit maintenance.
