Introduction

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) integrates mindfulness practices with cognitive strategies to alleviate perceived stress and its associated symptoms. While well studied in adults, evidence for schoolbased MBCT among adolescents with psychosomatic complaints—particularly in China—remains limited.

Methods

We conducted a quasi-experimental, mixed-methods study in three public secondary schools in DaZhou (Sichuan, China). Female students aged 14–16 were screened (N = 1,200) using the Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS; cutoff > 45) and DSM-5–informed clinical interviews; 60 eligible students were randomly allocated to MBCT or school-as-usual control. The primary outcomes were the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS; α = 0.85) and the PCS (α = 0.86). Parent (n = 6) and teacher (n = 5) interviews were analyzed thematically.

Results

Compared with controls, the MBCT group showed greater reductions in perceived stress and psychosomatic symptoms at post-test and two-month follow-up (Group×Time, p < .001; partial η²≈0.32–0.63). Mean reductions exceeded a conventional 0.5 SD benchmark, supporting applied relevance alongside statistical significance.

Conclusion

School-delivered MBCT was associated with sustained improvements in perceived stress and psychosomatic complaints among 14–16-year-old girls, with convergent qualitative evidence from parents and teachers. Interpretation remains cautious given the passive control, short follow-up, and bounded generalizability.

This study evaluates a school-based MBCT program for Chinese adolescents aged 14–16 with psychosomatic symptoms, testing its effectiveness in reducing perceived stress and improving mental health. Outcomes are triangulated using adolescent self-reports and streamlined evaluations from parents and teachers to establish ecological validity within everyday school and home environments.

H1 MBCT produces a significant Group (MBCT vs. control) × Time (baseline, post-test, follow-up) interaction on the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and the Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS), with greater reductions in the MBCT group.
H2 After adjustment for baseline (and prespecified covariates), the MBCT group shows lower PSS and PCS means than the control group at post-test and follow-up.
H3 A higher proportion of MBCT participants achieves clinically meaningful improvement on PSS and PCS (e.g., ≥ 0.5 SD reduction or reliable change) than controls.
H4 = parents and teachers report improvements in students’ emotion regulation, coping, classroom functioning, and management of somatic complaints after MBCT relative to control.

An a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1) indicated that 26 participants per group provide 0.80 power to detect a medium effect (f = 0.25) at α = 0.05; thus, the randomized sample is adequate. Three participants withdrew (two experimental, one control), leaving 57 completers (28 experimental, 29 control). Eligibility required: PCS > 45; no current psychiatric medication/psychotherapy; age 14–16 years; student assent and guardian consent; and commitment to session attendance. Exclusion criteria were > 3 absences, disqualifying medical conditions, or refusal to complete follow-up. Group means did not differ at baseline on key measures.

The Psychosomatic Complaints Scale (PCS) consists of 30 items, each scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 90. A cutoff score of 45 indicates potential psychosomatic dysfunction. Initial studies reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.64 and confirmed a unifactorial structure with one factor explaining 34.1% of the variance. Hajloo [63] confirmed its one-dimensionality (KMO = 0.782) and high reliability (α = 0.85; r = .85 for test–retest reliability). In the present study, the PCS underwent further psychometric evaluation, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 and acceptable model fit indices in confirmatory factor analysis (CFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.06), thus empirically supporting its reliability and validity for the Chinese adolescent sample.

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) consists of 14 items, each rated on a 5-point scale (0 = “never” to 4 = “very often”), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 56. Internal consistency in the original development sample ranged from 0.84 to 0.86 [65]. Broton et al. [42] achieved acceptable reliability (α = 0.81 in the Iranian sample and 0.86 in the American sample). The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.85, confirming satisfactory internal consistency. A confirmatory factor analysis further validated the two-factor structure of the PSS (CFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.05).

Attrition was low (three withdrawals), and an attrition analysis showed no baseline differences between completers and non-completers. For quantitative outcomes, we used mixed ANOVA (Group × Time) with assumption checks and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons, reporting partial η² and confidence intervals. Missingness was low (< 5%) and passed Little’s MCAR test; nevertheless, given known biases of LOCF, we used multiple imputation as our primary strategy: m = 20 datasets via fully conditional specification (predictive mean matching for continuous variables), including Group, Time, outcomes at all waves, and covariates in the imputation model. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. We performed LOCF and complete-case analyses as sensitivity checks; the conclusions remained unchanged across both approaches.

For perceived stress, students in the experimental group reported a significant reduction from pre-test (M = 35.33, SD = 6.12, 95% CI [33.01, 37.65]) to post-test (M = 28.55, SD = 6.58, 95% CI [26.06, 31.04]). Although scores slightly increased at follow-up (M = 29.50, SD = 6.46, 95% CI [27.06, 31.94]), they remained well below baseline levels. By contrast, the control group showed no meaningful change across time points (pre-test: M = 33.42, SD = 4.79, 95% CI [31.77, 35.07]; post-test: M = 34.36, SD = 4.49, 95% CI [32.82, 35.90]; follow-up: M = 34.05, SD = 4.58, 95% CI [32.49, 35.61]). A similar pattern emerged for mental health (where lower scores reflect fewer symptoms). The experimental group improved from pre-test (M = 55.83, SD = 7.94, 95% CI [52.71, 58.95]) to post-test (M = 49.66, SD = 7.53, 95% CI [46.70, 52.62]) and maintained this gain at follow-up (M = 49.94, SD = 7.58, 95% CI [46.97, 52.91]). In contrast, the control group’s mental health scores remained virtually unchanged (pre-test: M = 57.21, SD = 6.55, 95% CI [54.93, 59.49]; post-test: M = 57.84, SD = 6.67, 95% CI [55.53, 60.15]; follow-up: M = 57.94, SD = 6.85, 95% CI [55.58, 60.30]). Applying the same ≥ 0.5 SD benchmark (≈ 3.97 on this scale), the experimental group’s mean reductions (pre→post Δ = 6.17; pre→follow-up Δ = 5.89) met or exceeded the criterion, supporting applied interpretability of the changes.

To test for statistical significance, a mixed-design ANOVA (2 × 3) was conducted (Table 3). For perceived stress, there was a significant main effect of time, F(2, 112) = 35.75, p < .0001, partial η² = 0.50, 95% CI [0.36, 0.60]; a significant main effect of group, F(1, 56) = 28.42, p < .0001, partial η² = 0.48, 95% CI [0.31, 0.58]; and a highly significant time × group interaction, F(2, 112) = 59.96, p < .0001, partial η² = 0.63, 95% CI [0.51, 0.70]. Adjusted mixed-effects models that controlled for baseline PSS/PCS, age, and school replicated all inferences, and multiple-imputation–pooled estimates converged with complete-case and LOCF sensitivity analyses (see Appendix), strengthening robustness.

Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons (Table 4) confirmed that perceived stress significantly decreased between pre-test and post-test (Mean Difference = 2.91, p = .001, 95% CI [1.88, 3.94]) and between pre-test and follow-up (Mean Difference = 2.60, p = .001, 95% CI [1.55, 3.65]); the small increase between post-test and follow-up (Mean Difference = − 0.31, p = .21) was not significant, indicating sustained stress reduction. For mental health, significant improvements were found from pre-test to post-test (Mean Difference = 2.76, p = .001, 95% CI [1.72, 3.80]) and from pre-test to follow-up (Mean Difference = 2.57, p = .001, 95% CI [1.48, 3.66]), with no significant change between post-test and follow-up (Mean Difference = − 0.19, p = .75). Within-group Bonferroni tests indicated that time contrasts were significant for the experimental group but not for the control group, adding precision to the interpretation that change was intervention-linked rather than due to measurement fluctuation.

These findings collectively confirm that MBCT led to significant and sustained improvements in both perceived stress and mental health among students with psychosomatic symptoms. The lack of substantial differences between post-test and follow-up scores supports the durability of the intervention’s effects over two months. Average reductions exceeded a half–standard deviation on both outcomes, a commonly used distribution-based marker of clinically meaningful change. No participant attrition occurred between post-test and follow-up, ensuring consistency in sample size and enhancing the reliability of longitudinal comparisons.

Thematic analysis of interviews with 11 informants (6 parents and 5 teachers) yielded six overarching themes. Teacher 4 noted that “two students still avoid class participation under stress,” suggesting that MBCT may require adaptation for those with more entrenched anxiety patterns or social withdrawal tendencies. Parent 2 noted, “Although my child is more selfaware, I wouldn’t say they’re emotionally resilient yet—it’s a work in progress.” Teacher 5 pointed out, “Some students remain socially reserved despite the intervention,” indicating that while MBCT may enhance social-emotional awareness, individual personality factors, such as introversion or past social experiences, may moderate its impact. Parent 6 remarked, “The academic improvements are there, but they’re slower compared to changes in mood or behavior.” Teacher 2 noted challenges in habit formation: “Some students say they forget to use the techniques when stressed, which shows that reinforcement might still be needed.” Parent 6 admitted, “He used to meditate daily, but now only does it when reminded.”

The findings suggest that MBCT had a significant positive impact on reducing perceived stress and improving mental health, aligning with previous studies supporting mindfulness interventions for psychological distress [1, 2]. In applied terms, the observed mean reductions exceeded a conventional half-standard-deviation benchmark in this sample, which strengthens the case for clinical relevance; however, this threshold is heuristic and should be interpreted cautiously alongside confidence intervals. However, the small sample size, quasi-experimental design, and lack of an active control group limit the generalizability of these findings. These factors, alongside the absence of blinding, may have contributed to potential placebo effects; therefore, future studies should incorporate larger, randomized controlled trials to isolate the specific effects of MBCT more effectively. Specifically, expectancy and attention effects could inflate between-group contrasts when controls receive less contact; detection bias is also plausible when assessors are not fully blinded. These risks may bias estimates upward, implying that our effects represent an upper bound under these design constraints.

The primary outcome of reduced perceived stress found in this study corroborates findings by Porcelli et al. [3]. The findings of this study are also consistent with recent evidence supporting the effectiveness of mindfulness-based interventions in reducing psychological distress and improving emotion regulation. For example, Feng et al. [67] demonstrated that mindfulness significantly reduced parenting stress among parents of children with autism, with resilience and psychological flexibility mediating this effect. Similarly, Zhang et al. [68] showed that mindfulness helped Chinese university students manage academic stress and improve emotional intelligence. A meta-analysis by Ng et al. [69] confirmed that mindfulness-based interventions are highly effective in reducing psychological symptoms in high school adolescents. Additionally, Lan et al. [70] found that mindfulness combined with life-skills training helped improve emotion regulation and reduce anxiety symptoms among Chinese migrant children.

The second significant finding concerns the improvement in mental health outcomes for students with psychosomatic disorders. This aligns with findings from Lan et al. [9], Petrenko et al. [2], and Martin et al. [11]. While these findings are significant, the absence of a placebo or active control group, as well as the lack of blinding in this study, are substantial limitations that could affect the robustness of the conclusions. Moreover, the relative stability of the control group could reflect valid invariance, regression to the mean, or minor expectancy effects from administrative contact; an attention-matched or education-based comparator would help disentangle specific from non-specific benefits.

In conclusion, this study contributes valuable insights into the potential of MBCT to reduce stress and improve mental health among adolescents with psychosomatic symptoms. By integrating both quantitative outcomes and qualitative insights from parents and teachers, the research offers a comprehensive view of the impact of MBCT on adolescents’ well-being. However, the study’s limitations, such as the small sample size, the quasi-experimental design, and the lack of an active control group, suggest that these findings should be interpreted with caution. Generalizability is bounded by setting (three public schools in DaZhou), age (14–16), and gender (female), and the design likely overestimates effects relative to fully blinded, attention-matched RCTs. To strengthen the validity of future research, studies should utilize randomized controlled trials, include larger and more diverse samples, and incorporate extended follow-up periods to assess the durability of the intervention’s effects. Specifically, we advocate for multi-arm trials comparing MBCT with an attention-matched health education control, preregistered mediator analyses (decentering, rumination, resilience), and moderator analyses (baseline avoidance, practice dose, instructor fidelity), as well as cost-effectiveness assessments, to inform school policy.
